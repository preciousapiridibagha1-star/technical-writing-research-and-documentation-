# Research Methodology and Evidence Standard

## 1. Purpose

This document defines a repeatable approach for producing credible research briefs, technology reviews and policy-oriented analysis.

## 2. Define the research question

A useful research question specifies:
- The issue being investigated.
- The population, sector or geography.
- The relevant timeframe.
- The decision or understanding the research should support.

Avoid questions so broad that the evidence cannot be evaluated consistently.

## 3. Set scope and definitions

Before collecting sources, record:
- Included and excluded topics.
- Key terms and how they are used.
- Geographic and time boundaries.
- Any assumptions that shape the enquiry.

If a term has different meanings across sources, explain which definition is being used.

## 4. Source strategy

Use sources according to the claim being checked:

| Source type | Appropriate use | Limitations to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Official government or regulator | Laws, policy, official statistics and programme rules | May not explain implementation experience |
| Original research paper | Methods, data and scholarly findings | May have narrow samples or dated results |
| International organisation | Cross-country data and policy analysis | Definitions and coverage may vary |
| Company documentation | Product features, pricing terms and technical specifications | Vendor claims require independent evaluation |
| Reputable journalism | Recent events, interviews and reported developments | Verify important claims against primary sources |
| Community discussion | User experiences and emerging issues | Anecdotal and not representative |

## 5. Evidence collection

Maintain a source log with:
- Author or organisation.
- Title.
- Publication date.
- URL or document identifier.
- Date accessed.
- Source type.
- Relevant claim or passage.
- Geographic and methodological scope.
- Reliability concerns.

For important claims, seek corroboration. Where reliable sources disagree, describe the disagreement rather than silently choosing the most convenient result.

## 6. Analysis

Separate:
- **Finding:** what the source or data directly shows.
- **Interpretation:** what the finding may mean.
- **Recommendation:** what action may follow.
- **Uncertainty:** what remains unknown.

Do not imply causation from descriptive or correlational evidence. Check whether comparisons use consistent definitions, populations and time periods.

## 7. Research quality checks

Before publication, ask:
- Does each major claim have a supporting source?
- Is the source authoritative for that specific claim?
- Is the source current enough?
- Have conflicting findings been considered?
- Are figures and units represented accurately?
- Are limitations and missing data stated?
- Can a reader distinguish fact from judgement?
- Would another researcher be able to follow the method?

## 8. Research brief structure

1. Title and date.
2. Executive summary.
3. Research question and purpose.
4. Scope and definitions.
5. Method and source strategy.
6. Findings, organised by theme.
7. Analysis and implications.
8. Limitations and unanswered questions.
9. Conclusion or recommendations.
10. References and source log.

## 9. Ethical and responsible practice

Respect copyright, privacy and research ethics. Do not expose personal information unnecessarily. Do not fabricate interviews, datasets, quotations, citations or findings. Where AI tools assist with research or drafting, independently verify factual claims and disclose their use where the relevant programme or publication requires it.

## 10. Versioning

Record the date of research and review time-sensitive claims before reuse. Programme eligibility, application windows, prices, product capabilities and policy details can change.
