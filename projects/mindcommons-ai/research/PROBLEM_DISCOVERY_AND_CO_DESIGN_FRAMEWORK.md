# Mozilla Fellowship — SRHIN Problem Discovery & Co-Design Framework

**Research date:** 17 November 2026  
**Opportunity:** Mozilla Foundation Fellowship 2027–2028 — Track I Embedded, SRHIN  
**Status:** Proposed research and engagement framework. This is a self-directed preparation document, not an SRHIN-approved methodology, commissioned study, completed consultation or claim of community endorsement.  
**Relationship to MindCommons AI:** MindCommons is a provisional design hypothesis only. It is not a fixed solution, product commitment or assumption about what SRHIN needs.

---

## 1. Why this framework exists

The SRHIN host challenge raises a substantial question about mental-health data from African communities being used to develop AI without meaningful consent, community control or a fair share of benefits.

At the same time, SRHIN's public materials describe existing work on data collection, consent, lived-experience participation, ethics oversight, governance and culturally grounded mental-health AI. A credible response must understand both realities. It must not assume that SRHIN has no safeguards, repeat existing work, or arrive with a predetermined software product.

The purpose of this framework is to make the work disciplined:

1. Understand the challenge as SRHIN and affected communities define it.
2. Investigate the systems, practices, incentives and experiences behind it.
3. Diagnose the root causes and distinguish confirmed problems from assumptions.
4. Design possible interventions with the people affected and responsible for implementation.
5. Build and implement only what is justified, feasible and wanted.
6. Evaluate what changed, learn from the result and share findings responsibly.

**The central commitment is problem-first, community-informed and solution-flexible work.** Technology is one possible intervention, not the starting assumption.

## 2. Central research question

> **What structural, institutional, legal, technical and economic barriers prevent African communities from exercising meaningful control over how their data is collected, accessed, shared and used to develop AI systems, and what practical interventions can address those barriers while ensuring that communities participate in and benefit from the resulting innovation?**

This is the overarching research question guiding the preparation. It is not a claim that every listed barrier is present in SRHIN's programmes, nor that one fellowship project can solve the entire issue across Africa.

### Supporting questions

- What does meaningful community control mean in practice, and who has the authority to define it?
- At which points in a data lifecycle can control, consent, privacy or benefit-sharing break down?
- Which problems are caused by law or policy, institutional arrangements, technical architecture, economic incentives, unequal bargaining power, or gaps between them?
- What safeguards and processes already exist, how do they operate in practice, and where—if anywhere—do they fail or create friction?
- Which intervention could make a material, testable contribution within the fellowship period without duplicating existing work?

## 3. Six-stage working method

The stages are sequential enough to create discipline, but iterative in practice. Evidence from a later stage may require returning to an earlier one.

### Stage 1 — Understand

**Purpose:** Establish a shared understanding of the host challenge, the people affected, the intended outcomes and the boundaries of the fellowship contribution.

**Work:**
- Read the Mozilla RFP and SRHIN's public materials carefully.
- Understand SRHIN's programmes, existing governance bodies, research workflows and stated priorities.
- Identify the different groups involved: organisational leadership, research and technical teams, ethics/governance actors, community leaders, young people, people with lived experience, community health workers and other relevant partners.
- Separate the broad African data-governance problem from the specific problem SRHIN wants this embedded Fellow to address.

**Outputs:** Challenge brief; stakeholder and decision-authority map; evidence register; initial assumptions and unknowns.

**Gate:** Can we explain the challenge accurately without presenting assumptions as facts?

### Stage 2 — Investigate

**Purpose:** Learn how the relevant systems and experiences actually work, rather than relying only on public descriptions or our own interpretation.

**Work:**
- Review relevant policies, consent materials, governance documents, data-use agreements, workflows and technical documentation that SRHIN is willing and authorised to share.
- Conduct appropriately approved conversations with leadership, researchers, technical staff, governance representatives and community stakeholders.
- Trace selected real or safely simulated examples through the data lifecycle: collection, consent, access, sharing, model development, reuse, withdrawal, outputs and benefit commitments.
- Ask what works well, what is difficult, what is missing and what existing efforts should be protected.

**Outputs:** Interview notes; process maps; evidence-backed workflow descriptions; stakeholder perspectives; unresolved questions.

**Gate:** Do we have enough first-hand and documentary evidence to describe the current state, including differences in perspective?

### Stage 3 — Diagnose

**Purpose:** Identify the actual problem and its root causes, distinguishing symptoms, causes, constraints and consequences.

**Investigative dimensions:**

| Dimension | What to investigate |
|---|---|
| Structural and power | Who sets the terms? Who can refuse, negotiate, challenge or stop a use? Where are bargaining power and decision authority concentrated? |
| Institutional | Which organisations and roles are accountable? Are responsibilities clear? Where do hand-offs, incentives or capacity constraints create gaps? |
| Legal and policy | What do applicable laws, ethics approvals, consent terms, contracts and cross-border arrangements require? Where is interpretation or implementation uncertain? |
| Technical | What can systems record, restrict, trace, audit, delete or explain? What happens across exports, third parties, model training and derived artefacts? |
| Economic | Who funds, owns, commercialises or benefits from the data and resulting systems? What costs or incentives shape decisions? |
| Community and lived experience | How do people understand the process? Can they exercise meaningful choice? What do they consider fair, safe, useful and beneficial? |

**Outputs:** Root-cause analysis; validated problem statement; evidence/confidence ratings; prioritised needs; explicit account of what is already working.

**Gate:** Can each proposed problem be supported by evidence from relevant people or records, and is it important enough to address?

### Stage 4 — Design

**Purpose:** Co-design and compare possible responses to the validated problem.

**Possible intervention classes (not recommendations in advance):**
- Governance or policy changes.
- Improved consent, communication or community decision processes.
- Data-use agreements, accountability and benefit-sharing arrangements.
- Training, capacity-building or institutional coordination.
- Changes to existing technical systems or workflows.
- A small new software component, only where a specific technical need is demonstrated.
- A combination of technical and non-technical interventions.

**Design criteria:**
- Responds to a problem validated by SRHIN and affected stakeholders.
- Respects existing governance and does not duplicate effective systems.
- Gives appropriate weight to community authority and lived experience.
- Minimises collection and exposure of sensitive data.
- Is feasible within the fellowship's time, budget and access constraints.
- Has clear ownership, maintenance and accountability after the pilot.
- Can be evaluated with meaningful evidence.

**Outputs:** Options appraisal; co-design records; chosen intervention rationale; requirements; safeguards; implementation and evaluation plan.

**Gate:** Have the relevant stakeholders shaped the design, and can we explain why this option is preferable to doing nothing, improving an existing process or choosing a non-technical intervention?

### Stage 5 — Build and implement

**Purpose:** Deliver the smallest useful intervention that the evidence and stakeholders justify.

**Work:**
- Agree scope, roles, permissions, success criteria and safeguards with the host.
- Prototype and test with synthetic or otherwise explicitly authorised data first.
- Integrate with existing systems only with permission and appropriate technical/security review.
- Pilot with the intended users under agreed ethics, consent, privacy and safeguarding arrangements.
- Document limitations, operational responsibilities, training and maintenance.

**Outputs:** Tested intervention or documented non-software deliverable; implementation record; user feedback; technical and operational documentation.

**Gate:** Is the intervention safe, useful, accepted by the responsible stakeholders and supportable beyond a demonstration?

### Stage 6 — Evaluate and share

**Purpose:** Determine whether the intervention addressed the validated problem, what did not work, and what should happen next.

**Work:**
- Evaluate against baseline and agreed success measures.
- Gather feedback from community participants and operational users.
- Assess unintended effects, burden, exclusions, privacy risks and whether decision-making power actually changed.
- Record what should be continued, modified, scaled or stopped.
- Share reusable learning responsibly, respecting confidentiality, consent, data rights and community preferences.

**Outputs:** Evaluation report; lessons learned; evidence of use or adoption where applicable; recommendations; responsibly shareable documentation.

**Gate:** Can we demonstrate what changed, for whom, under what conditions and with what limitations?

## 4. Stakeholder discovery and question toolkit

The following questions are a preparation bank. They are not a rigid questionnaire. Questions should be adapted to each person's role, language, knowledge, consent and safeguarding needs. Ask open questions first; use probes only where they help clarify an answer.

### A. SRHIN leadership and programme leadership

1. What is the most important change SRHIN wants this fellowship to help achieve?
2. What specific situation or recurring problem led to this host challenge?
3. Which communities, programmes, data flows or AI-development activities are within scope—and which are not?
4. What would make the fellowship genuinely valuable to SRHIN after 12 months?
5. What has SRHIN already tried, and what has been learned?
6. Which decisions must remain with SRHIN, communities, ethics bodies or other partners?
7. What constraints should a Fellow understand before proposing an intervention?
8. What would count as an unacceptable outcome, even if a technical prototype worked?

### B. Research, data and technical teams

1. Could you walk me through the relevant data lifecycle from collection to use, sharing, model development and any later reuse?
2. At each stage, which system, document or person records the purpose, permissions and restrictions?
3. How are new purposes, new collaborators, exports or changed model-training uses assessed?
4. How are consent withdrawal and data-subject requests handled after data has been shared or used in model development?
5. What is technically traceable today, and what still requires manual coordination?
6. Which existing tools or workflows are effective and should not be replaced?
7. Where do staff experience repeated delays, uncertainty, duplicated work or accountability gaps?
8. What information could a Fellow safely inspect, and what must remain inaccessible?

### C. Governance, ethics and legal stakeholders

1. Who has authority to approve, refuse, condition or review each relevant data use?
2. How are community decisions represented in formal institutional decisions and agreements?
3. How are disagreements, complaints, appeals and escalations handled?
4. How are onward sharing, cross-border transfers, retention, deletion and secondary use governed?
5. What obligations apply to external partners, vendors, researchers and downstream users?
6. How are benefit-sharing commitments defined, documented, monitored and reviewed?
7. Which existing policies or mechanisms are working well, and where are their limits?
8. What legal, ethical or governance advice would be required before a pilot?

### D. Community leaders, young people and people with lived experience

These conversations require appropriate safeguarding, accessible language, voluntary participation and a clear explanation that participation will not affect access to services or benefits.

1. When organisations collect information from people in your community, what do people understand about what happens to it?
2. What information do people feel comfortable sharing, and what would make them uncomfortable?
3. What does meaningful permission or consent look like from your perspective?
4. What should a community be able to decide about how its information is used?
5. Who should represent the community in those decisions, and how should that representation be accountable?
6. What should happen if people disagree with a proposed use?
7. What would make people feel respected, safe and informed throughout the process?
8. What benefits would the community itself consider meaningful?
9. How should the community know whether promises have been kept?
10. What should researchers or technology developers never do with community data?

### E. Community health workers and frontline practitioners

1. How are people informed about data collection and its purpose in actual practice?
2. What questions or concerns do participants commonly raise?
3. What makes consent conversations difficult in real settings?
4. What happens when someone changes their mind or asks for information to be corrected or withdrawn?
5. What additional workload would a proposed process create?
6. What tools, training or support would make the process practical?
7. What feedback reaches the community after data has been collected or used?

### F. Mentors and external specialists

1. Which assumptions in the current problem framing need stronger evidence?
2. What comparable approaches or prior work should be examined?
3. What could be technically feasible but institutionally or ethically inappropriate?
4. What is a realistic fellowship-sized contribution?
5. What risks or unintended consequences are easy to overlook?
6. What evidence would convince a critical reviewer that the problem and intervention are well grounded?

## 5. Evidence capture and research records

A disciplined record is essential. Each material claim should be traceable to its source and status.

For every interview or workshop, record—only with appropriate permission:
- Date, format and participant role (use a pseudonym or role label where appropriate).
- Purpose of the conversation and the consent/notice provided.
- Questions asked and a faithful summary of responses.
- Direct quotations only where specifically permitted and safely de-identified.
- Points of agreement, disagreement and uncertainty.
- Documents or workflows mentioned, and whether they were actually reviewed.
- Follow-up actions and who is responsible.
- Whether the participant has approved any quotation or attribution intended for publication.

Maintain a separate evidence register with these labels:

- **Public-source fact:** supported by an accessible public source.
- **Host-confirmed information:** confirmed by an authorised SRHIN representative.
- **Community perspective:** expressed by a participant; do not generalise it to all community members.
- **Observed workflow:** directly observed with permission.
- **Researcher interpretation:** an analysis that should be tested.
- **Unverified hypothesis:** a question or possible explanation, not a finding.

### Recording, transcription and AI-assisted analysis safeguards

Recording is not automatic. Obtain informed permission before recording; explain purpose, access, storage, retention, transcription and intended AI-assisted analysis. Offer a meaningful non-recorded option where feasible. Do not record minors or sensitive mental-health information without the specific ethics, safeguarding, guardian/assent and host approvals that apply. Follow SRHIN's requirements and applicable law.

Do not upload identifiable recordings, transcripts, health information or confidential organisational material to a third-party AI service unless the host has explicitly authorised that exact processing arrangement and the required privacy, security, legal and ethics checks are complete. Prefer approved transcription environments, data minimisation and de-identification. Remove identifiers where possible, restrict access, keep source files separate from analysis copies, and document the tools and processing steps. AI-generated summaries are working aids, not authoritative records: check them against the source, preserve uncertainty and disagreements, and never allow a model to invent quotations or participant views.

Community members and stakeholders should be able to understand how their contributions will be used and, where agreed, review quotations or attributed material before publication.

## 6. Initial hypotheses to investigate — not conclusions

The following are possible explanations suggested by the broad challenge. They must be tested rather than treated as established facts about SRHIN:

- **Power imbalance:** communities may have limited bargaining power compared with institutions, funders, vendors or AI developers.
- **Fragmented accountability:** responsibility may be distributed across data collectors, custodians, researchers, vendors and downstream users.
- **Consent limitations:** consent to initial collection may not, by itself, settle every future use, recipient or model-development scenario.
- **Lifecycle visibility:** later reuse, derivative datasets, model artefacts or onward sharing may be difficult for communities to see or influence.
- **Legal/institutional gaps:** formal rights or policies may not translate into accessible, enforceable processes.
- **Economic asymmetry:** communities may contribute data while value, revenue, infrastructure or decision-making power accumulates elsewhere.
- **Capacity and resourcing:** meaningful participation, independent advice, governance and long-term monitoring require time and resources.
- **Communication barriers:** technical or legal explanations may not be accessible in the languages and formats communities prefer.

Each hypothesis should be marked supported, contradicted, partly supported or unresolved, with evidence and stakeholder perspectives recorded.

## 7. Intervention options — keep the solution open

After diagnosis, compare at least these categories:

1. **No new intervention:** the existing process is sufficient; document evidence and recommend maintaining it.
2. **Improve an existing process:** clarify responsibilities, documentation, communication, training or escalation.
3. **Governance or agreement intervention:** improve policy, contracts, community decision processes or benefit-sharing arrangements.
4. **Capacity and participation intervention:** resource community understanding, representation, negotiation or oversight.
5. **Technical enhancement:** improve an existing platform or workflow rather than create a parallel one.
6. **Small new tool or prototype:** only if a validated need cannot be met proportionately through existing systems or non-technical changes.
7. **Combined intervention:** address connected institutional, community and technical causes together.

### Decision test before committing to a build

Do not begin implementation until the following can be answered:

- What precise problem has been validated?
- Who experiences it, and who has authority to confirm the need?
- What evidence demonstrates the problem?
- What existing process or tool already addresses it?
- Why is that response insufficient?
- What non-software alternatives were considered?
- Who will own, operate and maintain the intervention?
- What sensitive data, permissions and approvals are involved?
- How will the intervention improve community agency or outcomes?
- How will success and possible harm be evaluated?

## 8. Relationship to the existing SRHIN assessment and MindCommons hypothesis

The [SRHIN Technical and Governance Gap Assessment](SRHIN_TECHNICAL_AND_GOVERNANCE_GAP_ASSESSMENT.md) documents what public sources currently say about SRHIN's work and identifies questions that cannot be answered from public evidence.

This framework adds the next layer: a method for discovering the host's actual priority and validating the problem with relevant stakeholders.

MindCommons AI remains one possible hypothesis arising from the desk assessment. It may be narrowed, redesigned, replaced by a non-software intervention or dropped entirely. No feature should be treated as a requirement until the relevant problem and users have been validated.

## 9. What this preparation does—and does not—claim

This document demonstrates a proposed research approach and the questions to be investigated. It does not claim that:
- SRHIN has approved this framework or selected the applicant.
- Interviews, workshops or community consultations have taken place.
- The listed root causes have been established in SRHIN's programmes.
- A specific technical gap has been confirmed.
- MindCommons is the required or preferred solution.
- Any tool has been deployed, adopted or shown to create impact.

The work remains self-directed public-source preparation until an authorised host engagement and appropriate approvals exist.

## 10. Immediate next step

Use this framework to prepare for a substantive discovery conversation with SRHIN. The first objective is not to pitch MindCommons. It is to ask the host to explain the intended outcome, the specific problem behind the challenge, the stakeholders whose perspectives matter, the existing work that must be understood, and the boundaries within which an embedded Fellow could contribute.

**Working principle:** Understand before diagnosing. Diagnose before designing. Co-design before building. Evaluate before claiming impact.
